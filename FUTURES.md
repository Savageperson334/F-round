# F-round — Not Necessarily a Game

A pile of **4,169 real GitHub repositories** is not a design document. It is a raw archaeological site full of intentionally incompatible fragments. The goal is not to turn all of them into dependencies. The goal is to expose unexpected relationships between human obsessions.

The catalog lives in [data/F_ROUND_MASTER_4100.csv](data/F_ROUND_MASTER_4100.csv). The interactive [index.html](index.html) can roll three very different entries and export a hypothesis. The larger catalog was built from broad GitHub searches: **SEARCH entries are leads, not inspected software**. Licenses and claims remain unverified unless inspected separately.

## Possible species of F-round

### 1. The Department of Unrelated Phenomena — a genuinely absurd game

Imagine being hired by an enormous government agency with no idea what its remit is. Its offices are organized by realities rather than departments: the Centre for Obsolete Typewriters, the Office of Bacterial Language, a Division of Hydrophone Ghosts, the Mechanical Dragonfly Desk, a Wreck Registry, a Cave Map Appeals Tribunal.

There are no arbitrary fantasy lore entries at first. Each discovery is based on an actual GitHub project, scientific procedure, dataset or niche mechanism; the game separates **what the source really is** from what our fictional agency thinks it is.

Gameplay is not reading 4,169 encyclopaedia pages. Every discovered mechanism produces or accepts something:
- a shape, path, image or spatial coordinate;
- an event or time series;
- a sound, phoneme, text stream or musical pattern;
- a piece of evidence, disputed historical claim, or probability;
- a physical-machine instruction.

Players use adapters to route one system into another. Translate a weather-fax image into an embroidery pattern, feed an artificial fungus's network topology into a spoken language, give a cave survey the voice of a formant synthesizer, convert a simulated weather event into a bureaucratic receipt. Some contraptions solve puzzles; others give new tools, new paths, or unexpected emergent behavior.

**Core design tension:** A delightfully strange output must not be mistaken for a genuine scientific or historical claim. Fiction is marked as fiction; factual descriptions link to real source repositories.

**Prototype without any imported repo code:** a tiny top-down office with eight rooms, each holding a curated input/output mechanism, a source terminal and one combinatorial interaction. Each room's real-world mechanism must be understandable in 30 seconds.

### 2. A Museum That Can Be Rewired

A walking-simulator/art museum whose exhibits are software processes rather than 3D sculptures. Every exhibit has a real cited origin. You can rewire its observations into another exhibition, then exhibit the transformed result.

This could become a collaboration platform for scientists, musicians, teachers and artists. No need for a quest system, avatar or game economy.

### 3. The Invention Roulette

A serious-but-fun research assistant that intentionally picks **distant** open-source parts and proposes a bridging input/output contract. It records:
- what each project actually does;
- whether the code, data and models are legally reusable;
- what might be generated or learned;
- the smallest standalone proof of interoperability;
- evidence that the experiment worked, or why it failed.

The existing collision browser is the deliberately primitive zero-dependency ancestor of this.

### 4. Atlas of Obsessions

A visual map of small software projects as an anthropological subject. Not popularity. Group authors' curiosities by the **verbs** their software performs: listening, splitting, measuring, folding, tracking, converting, hallucinating, visualizing, filtering, simulating.

A cave-map app might sit next to a nuclear particle tracking system because both turn paths into evidence. A receipt printer might sit next to a thermal imaging script because both turn invisible values into monochrome images.

A very unusual educational interface, and useful for discovering adjacent technologies.

### 5. The Physical Nonsense Printer

Take a factual or synthetic event from any source and print it as a small object: an SVG, receipt, embroidery map, music score, e-ink frame, or folded pattern. Every output carries provenance and the transformation history.

This is a toolkit for installation artists, teachers, and anyone who loves anachronistic machinery.

### 6. A Synthetic Planet of Real Algorithms

Instead of combining libraries into an app, use their verified algorithms as **ecological laws**. Actual fluid simulation describes moving water; actual L-systems grow vegetation; actual acoustic processing interprets imaginary wildlife; graph layouts represent social relations; historical boundary data informs collapsed societies.

The world would be surreal because its laws are unexpectedly heterogeneous, not because a writer simply invented random events.

**Danger:** An algorithm found in a repository is not necessarily validated physical science. Real mechanisms and artistic rules must remain distinguishable.

### 7. A Radio Station Transmitting the Entire Archive

Once per episode, a handful of actual obscure projects are introduced via mini documentaries, fake transmissions clearly labelled fiction, short sounds, visually coded charts and links to the original software.

The station eventually develops characters and recurring systems from the places where the software overlaps. A lovely odd medium that doesn't require building a huge game at all.

### 8. The Missing-Connector Atlas

A genuine tool for researchers: catalogue the inputs and outputs of tiny projects, then expose edges where a **minimal adapter** would make them compatible. An unknown 30-line script might be the missing piece between two powerful applications. No genre or business category; the unit is a transformation.

## Two classification systems, both useful

**Topic shelves** help you search: geochemistry, teletext, insects, robotics, archaeology, synths, embroidery, etc.

**Mechanism tags** help you build: `audio -> events`, `raster -> geometry`, `signal -> text`, `time series -> audio`, `geometry -> machine commands`, `graph -> grammar`, `coordinates -> art`.

The game-like potential lives in **mechanisms**. Themes alone produce weird trivia. Mechanisms produce unpredictable interactions.

## Experimental ground rules

- Discovery is not endorsement or functionality proof. A GitHub search hit can be a class exercise, broken prototype, license-free public code, or an active research project.
- Do not run untrusted code with personal credentials, filesystem or network access. Scout read-only first; sandbox execution later.
- Code, assets, models, databases and audio recordings can have different reuse conditions. Public is not automatically open source.
- Keep an explicit difference between simulated/fictional data, real observations, tentative machine classifications and established facts.
- No fixed product concept yet. We are collecting strange laws of reality.

**The first milestone is not a game. It is a map of possibilities interesting enough that no one can predict the next experiment.**
