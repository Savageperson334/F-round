# F-round

> **Everything is potentially connected. Especially the things that absolutely should not be.**

**F-round is an open-ended expedition through the strange things people have made.** Forgotten software. Scientific instruments. Obsessive single-purpose utilities. Glorious one-person projects. Broken experiments. Mathematical curiosities. Biology, chemistry, geography, history, radios, extinct media formats, mechanical devices, generative art, ridiculous simulations, and phenomena nobody had a good reason to turn into software.

There is no prescribed genre, industry, platform, aesthetic, business model, or final product.

There are, however, **two major concepts worth developing in parallel**:

- **A. An absurd but substantial game:** an explorable, interconnected universe of real ideas, improbable systems, ridiculous institutions, and consequences that get increasingly out of hand.
- **B. A practical invention workshop:** an engine for discovering, adapting, connecting, and making genuinely useful or fascinating things out of unrelated open-source projects, datasets, algorithms, and hardware.

Both may become enormous. Both may change beyond recognition. One might eventually consume the other. Neither is merely a prototype for the other.

**We are collecting the pieces before deciding what the machine is.**

---

## The raw material: thousands of tiny obsessions

GitHub is not just a warehouse of web frameworks. Somewhere there is a person who wrote a tool to operate a specific antique typewriter. Someone else is simulating colonies of bacteria, mapping underwater caves, recognizing whale calls, turning measurements into speech, reconstructing vanished national borders, printing weather reports on receipt paper, or generating instructions for a mechanical loom.

That is the material of F-round.

The first research expedition collected **4,169 distinct GitHub repository leads across 63 research shelves** (snapshot: November 22, 2026). These are real repository links, **not 4,169 validated or legally reusable software components**. Every row now carries GitHub's own metadata (description, topics, language, licence ID, stars, dates), but only an initial 120 scouted projects have been looked at by anyone; most entries are still uninvestigated leads.

Start with the **[4,169-repository master CSV](data/F_ROUND_MASTER_4100.csv)**, its [catalogue explanation](CATALOG.md), or the [offline collision browser](index.html). The browser can randomly combine entries, lock interesting discoveries, search categories, and export speculative experiment briefs.

### Anything is allowed into the search

Astronomy, weird chemistry, procedural ecosystems, medieval records, laboratory automation, typography, obscure programming languages, machine embroidery, hardware archaeology, music, hydrophones, fossils, visual mathematics, cave surveys, atmospheric telemetry, tiny robots, software for forgotten machines, strange food graphs, satellites, insects, 3D reconstruction, non-human communication, radio protocols, obscure games, and subjects we have not imagined yet.

**Popularity is not a selection criterion.** A nearly abandoned repository with one star can be more inspiring than a polished library with fifty thousand.

A project can be useful in five different ways: as **working code**, as **data**, as a **documented method**, as a **mechanism worth independently reimplementing**, or simply as a **strange idea that changes our direction**.

# Concept A — The Game

## A serious game with absolutely no respect for reasonable boundaries

**Working description:** A deeply interactive, tonally unhinged game about discovering how a world made of incompatible ideas actually works.

Not a random-meme simulator. Not a slideshow of weird GitHub repositories. Not an encyclopedia with quests stapled onto it.

We want something that can feel intelligent, atmospheric, mechanically rewarding, emotionally surprising, profoundly stupid, occasionally unsettling, and genuinely beautiful—sometimes within the same five minutes.

Imagine a world where an archaeological department studies obsolete computer protocols as if they were fossils; a civil service processes reports sent by migratory birds; a laboratory grows bureaucratic fungal networks; an entire municipal district depends on a forgotten weatherfax transmission; the transport authority has confused astronomical orbits with street addresses; and an experimental phonetics machine has begun assigning voices to caves.

The absurdity is fiction. **The inspiration underneath comes from real tools, methods, histories, datasets, or mechanisms.**

### The player is not just sightseeing

The fundamental fantasy is **learning a strange rule, then doing something consequential with it**.

A possible gameplay rhythm:

1. **Encounter:** Find a mechanism, object, institution, ecosystem, document, machine, or peculiar situation.
2. **Investigate:** Discover what it does, what it accepts, what it produces, and what its limitations are.
3. **Experiment:** Connect it to something it was never designed to interact with.
4. **Observe consequences:** The output changes a system, solves or creates a problem, uncovers a relationship, mutates an environment, or opens another possibility.
5. **Carry the discovery forward:** Tools, knowledge, connections, and consequences persist, allowing increasingly improbable interactions.

The fun should not depend on knowing a particular scientific discipline. The game teaches the player through interaction, surprise, failure, and play.

### The world does not need a single sensible premise

The **Department of Unrelated Phenomena** is one possible framing: a giant institution attempting to administer a reality it clearly does not understand. But this is **a candidate setting, not a settled narrative**.

Other possibilities include a discontinuous city, a wandering museum, an interconnected research complex, an impossible natural world, an absurd post-apocalyptic simulation, a collection of self-contained regions that slowly infect one another, or something not yet named.

Similarly, we have **not chosen** first-person versus isometric, 2D versus 3D, discrete levels versus a seamless world, procedural generation versus authored content, solo versus multiplayer, or any specific engine.

The game must earn its shape from what actually becomes fun to do.

### Gameplay pillars

**1. Real mechanisms; unreasonable combinations.** A graph algorithm, a formant synth, a simulated fluid, and a historical map should not be interchangeable decorations. Their *actual differences* are what make combinations surprising. One thing generates paths, another understands paths, another speaks when paths change.

**2. Deep, systemic play.** If the player teaches a machine to interpret something differently, that interpretation should have consequences beyond a single scripted joke. Aim for chains of cause and effect, layered puzzles, simulation, experimentation, discovery, and systems that collide in ways the designer did not manually enumerate.

**3. Radical variety.** A scene could be a puzzle, an instrument, an ecosystem, a machinery workshop, a detective problem, a bizarre social encounter, a data visualization, a tactical situation, or a quiet piece of exploration. No single mechanic must absorb every other idea.

**4. Tonal whiplash that is deliberate, not random.** Scientific rigor next to nonsense. Melancholy next to slapstick. Uncanny machinery next to genuinely silly dialogue. A terrible bureaucratic misunderstanding could reveal a real mathematical idea. A joke can become an unexpectedly consequential world event.

**5. Curiosity as progression.** Learnable methods, physical artifacts, tools, relationships, systems, and discovered rules can be more meaningful than simply collecting levels or stat points. Knowledge and experimentation should unlock new capabilities.

**6. A sense of an enormous world with hidden depth.** Some systems can be tiny and self-contained. Others may have dozens of interactions. The player should feel that the world continues beyond whatever is currently understood.

### Examples of possible game situations

These are **fictional design sketches**, not implemented mechanics:

- A cave-survey station can only send its map as a spoken transmission. Its voice is generated from tunnel geometry. When you alter a route, the message changes.
- A colony of artificial organisms develops a branching network. The planning department mistakes that network for a proposed railway and builds an entire administrative process around it.
- A derelict research office uses a receipt printer as its only memory. Every strange phenomenon generates a physical evidence slip that can be reclassified, forged *in fiction*, or combined with another record.
- A border dispute involves two historical maps that contradict one another. The conflict is not resolved by choosing the prettiest map; you investigate how records were produced and decide what the contradiction means for the world.
- A cryptography machine receives bird migration coordinates as its key material. The result is nonsense until another system reveals that the output is a path.
- A mechanical dragonfly becomes the interface for examining an immense organism whose body follows the rules of a simulated fluid.
- An entire district relies on software for a discontinued printer, and the player has to find a modern workaround using principles learned elsewhere.
- A perfectly serious scientist introduces a ridiculous project with immense pride. It turns out to be one of the most mechanically sophisticated things in the game.

Any of these could be a five-minute curiosity, a major quest, a simulation subsystem, a character's obsession, or something that is never used. **No example is a promise to implement it.**

### What keeps this from becoming arbitrary nonsense?

The distinction between **weird trivia** and **deep interaction**.

Trivia tells the player that some obscure device exists. A meaningful game lets the player understand a small part of how that device works, discover a new use for its output, and experience the consequences.

We do not need 4,169 working software integrations. We need a curated selection of **mechanisms that are legible, interesting, and capable of interacting**. Many source projects will supply only background research or inspiration.

The game's real-world source material should be discoverable through optional notes or credits. **Fiction, simulated data, uncertain scientific claims, and real historical information must never be presented as if they were the same thing.**

### Early game experiments, not a fixed roadmap

A small test area containing a few genuinely different interactive systems is more useful than immediately building a giant world. We could try:

- One room that generates data, one that transforms it, and one that interprets it incorrectly.
- A tiny ecological simulation whose state can affect a machine, an NPC, or a puzzle.
- A prototype of an institutional interface that is increasingly absurd but mechanically functional.
- An explorable historical contradiction with player-controlled investigation.
- A self-contained scene that is simultaneously a funny encounter and a usable instrument.

The only initial requirement: **something the player can do should be more interesting than merely reading about what inspired it**.

# Concept B — The Practical Invention Workshop

## Make things nobody thought to ask for

**Working description:** A completely open-ended system for discovering and building useful, beautiful, educational, experimental, or deeply peculiar tools by connecting unrelated repositories and methods.

This is **not** required to become a single app or a commercial platform. It could be a collection of small programs, experimental installations, research tools, hardware prototypes, data pipelines, libraries, artistic instruments, or independent finished products.

Some inventions may be serious enough for scientists or educators. Others may be tiny personal utilities. Some may be absurd but still produce a meaningful output. Some will be dead ends, and those failures can be informative.

**The practical branch does not have to justify itself by helping the game.**

### What might we make?

| Direction | Example output |
| --- | --- |
| Scientific and laboratory tools | Instrument viewers, measurement validation, unusual plotters, visual analysis, converters, or simulation frontends |
| Sound and experimental music | Data-driven synthesis, voices, sequencers, environmental sonification, or unfamiliar control systems |
| Geographic and historical research | Time-aware maps, conflicting-source explorers, visualization tools, or georeferencing utilities |
| Digital archaeology | Viewers and converters for obsolete file formats, computer interfaces, and forgotten media |
| Biological experiments | Tools to inspect recordings, microscopic patterns, growth rules, ecological observations, or simulated organisms |
| Physical-machine projects | Thermal-printer art, knitting and embroidery patterns, pen-plotter output, e-ink displays, or retro hardware interfaces |
| Education and public understanding | Interactive demonstrations of obscure scientific ideas or small research datasets |
| Creative software | Generative geometry, procedural writing, hybrid 2D/3D instruments, visual languages, and experimental interfaces |
| Research infrastructure | Reproducible adapters that allow two previously isolated tools or datasets to exchange information |
| Unclassified inventions | Anything for which none of these labels is adequate |

It is perfectly acceptable for a project to begin with: *"This tiny repository performs a weird calculation. What else could that calculation drive?"*

### A few credible starting collisions

- **Microscope to plotter:** Extract visual structure from openly reusable microscopy imagery, generate vector fields, and plot the result as line art. Keep scientific measurements separate from artistic transformations. Sources to investigate: [ParticleAnalyzer](https://github.com/rybakov-ks/ParticleAnalyzer), [Plotter-Studio](https://github.com/iclubu/Plotter-Studio).
- **Real-world observations to an unusual broadcast:** Turn a verified acoustic or environmental event into a sonified signal, coded image, or printed record. Sources: [OpenSoundscape](https://github.com/kitzeslab/opensoundscape), [pySSTV](https://github.com/dnet/pySSTV), [artery-thermal](https://github.com/nthmost/artery-thermal).
- **Historic maps with provenance:** Compare border datasets over time and attach contradictory map evidence instead of silently choosing a single truth. Sources: [historical-basemaps](https://github.com/aourednik/historical-basemaps), [MapWarper](https://github.com/timwaters/mapwarper), [Tropy](https://github.com/tropy/tropy).
- **An instrument that outputs something unexpected:** Feed synthetic or well-understood lab data into a scientific chart and a separate sonic or physical representation. Sources: [CHNOSZ](https://github.com/jedick/CHNOSZ), [strauss](https://github.com/james-trayford/strauss), [crosstitrate](https://github.com/ohlincha/crosstitrate).
- **Artificial growth to physical pattern:** Use graph growth or morphology rules to produce stitch paths, tiles, or printed drawings. Sources: [rewriting_systems](https://github.com/mundyreimer/rewriting_systems), [Ink/Stitch](https://github.com/inkstitch/inkstitch). A usable version must independently verify the geometry and output requirements.

These examples are **proposals for experiments**, not claims that the named projects already integrate, that their dependencies run together, or that every codebase has a reusable license.

### Practical means an outcome, not necessarily a market

Useful things can be tiny. A file converter that saves one researcher an hour, an accessible visualization of an unfamiliar concept, a working interface to an obsolete machine, or an artist's instrument with an entirely new control surface can all be worthwhile.

A useful invention should do at least one of these things:

- Reveal information that would otherwise be difficult to perceive.
- Transform something between formats or physical media.
- Let people explore, compare, understand, or validate a difficult subject.
- Make a previously isolated tool or dataset accessible.
- Create an interesting artifact, instrument, or experience.
- Solve a real task in a new or strangely elegant way.

A product, business model, or customer may emerge **after** something proves valuable. Nothing in the scope requires monetization.

### The practical workflow

1. **Find:** Collect strange tools, algorithms, data, devices, and experiments without demanding an immediate reason.
2. **Understand:** Inspect what a candidate truly does—its inputs, outputs, limitations, dependencies, provenance, and rights.
3. **Collide:** Connect candidates through a concrete intermediate representation, even if they have no thematic relationship.
4. **Prove:** Make the smallest reproducible adapter or demonstration using synthetic or properly licensed inputs.
5. **Evaluate:** Determine whether the result is useful, expressive, surprising, educational, or merely interesting as a failed experiment.
6. **Keep, fork, contribute, or abandon:** A result might become its own project, improve an upstream repository, feed the game, or remain a documented curiosity.

Do not begin by dumping a thousand downloaded repositories into one giant dependency tree.

# What A and B share

**The same library of strange mechanisms can power two different ambitions.**

| Shared discovery | Game possibility (A) | Practical possibility (B) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cave survey geometry | A cave whose tunnels encode a spoken message | An accessible spatial sonification viewer |
| Bacterial growth rules | A persistent organism that learns bizarre paths | A teaching or research visualization |
| Historical mapping errors | A dispute that changes the playable world | A sourced map-error investigation tool |
| Formant synthesis | A machine with a strange, evolving voice | An experimental speech instrument |
| Thermal printing | Physical records used in investigation or puzzles | An automated event-to-receipt system |
| Vector field models | Traversal, navigation, or environmental simulation | Generative drawing and flow analysis |
| Obsolete file formats | Recovering fictional evidence in a dead system | Real format inspection and conversion |

Neither branch should reduce the other to a side feature.

If a good practical tool emerges, we can develop it on its own merits. If a weird game mechanic works, we can use the idea without importing an entire scientific library. Some discoveries belong only in our research notes.

# The shared foundation: a library of capabilities, not merely a list of links

The collection currently groups projects by **topics**: sonification, archaeology, hydrophones, geochemistry, graphics, robotics, maps, and so forth.

Longer term, the much more powerful classification is **what something actually does**.

Consider these conceptual transformations:

| From | Into | Possible result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Audio | Events or labels | Detected calls, signals, patterns |
| Timeseries | Sound | An audible measurement or evolving instrument |
| Raster image | Geometry | Contours, vector paths, or stitch instructions |
| Coordinates | A map or scene | Historical reconstruction, navigation, analysis |
| Graph | Grammar or movement | A branching narrative, path, or evolving structure |
| Text | Speech or encoded signal | Voice, strange broadcasts, machine instructions |
| Simulation state | Physical output | A plotted, printed, or otherwise tangible artifact |
| Observations | Evidence record | A sourced report that keeps its uncertainty |

These are **possible bridges**, not a universal protocol we have already implemented.

Once we understand capabilities rather than just project names, genuinely surprising connections become findable. It also gives the game meaningful interactions and the practical branch reusable adapters.

## A repository can be inspiration without being a dependency

Every candidate should eventually have some combination of:

- **Identity:** Repository name, source link, author, date, short purpose.
- **Evidence level:** Search result, overview inspected, source examined, test run, actual integration.
- **Capability:** Input, transformation, output, important constraints.
- **Practical conditions:** Language, environment, data formats, hardware, dependencies.
- **Legal conditions:** Code license, data/model/media rights, attribution and distribution constraints.
- **Possibilities:** Unexpected connections, candidate artifacts, game mechanics, useful outputs.
- **Experiment record:** What was actually attempted, what happened, and whether it can be reproduced.

We should not assign detailed capabilities to thousands of repositories just because they matched a search phrase. **Unknown is a legitimate field value.**

# Scope: deliberately enormous, intentionally unfinished

### In scope

Exploring any field; collecting obscure repositories; studying other people's ideas; experimenting with real scientific or historical material; inventing fictional systems; building game mechanics; creating unusual art; producing practical utilities; interacting with physical hardware; writing adapters; and allowing one experiment to spawn an entirely different project.

### Not yet decided

Genre. Art style. Game engine. Narrative structure. Commercial or noncommercial distribution. User audience. Primary programming language. Hardware platform. Whether the practical branch has a single interface. Whether the game is one world or many. Whether any of this becomes a product at all.

### Explicitly not required

- A thousand codebases running in one executable.
- A meaningful connection for every repository.
- A giant finished game before we can build useful small tools.
- A business plan, monetization strategy, or conventional startup pitch.
- A single coherent aesthetic across all experiments.
- A rule that every project must be active, popular, maintained, or even finished.
- A claim that every public GitHub project is open-source licensed or safe to execute.

**The search can be wildly undisciplined. Actual experiments should be reproducible and honest about what works.**

# How to work on F-round

This project may remain a library of discoveries while experiments grow around it. We do not need to lock in its folder structure or technology stack yet.

For a new idea, capture four things:

1. **The strange ingredients:** source links and what each candidate appears to do.
2. **The impossible question:** what we would find out by connecting them.
3. **The smallest proof:** one input, one transformation, one observable output.
4. **The honest result:** what happened, what failed, what is still assumed.

Useful shared formats might include CSV, JSON, SVG, GeoJSON, WAV, MIDI, raster images, and graph descriptions. But a small direct connection is sometimes better than building an abstract framework.

## Principles worth keeping as the scope expands

**Treat weirdness as valuable.** Do not filter out an idea because it sounds absurd.

**Take the underlying ideas seriously.** Nonsense built on understandable rules is more satisfying than random noise.

**Keep provenance.** A real measurement, an AI guess, a speculative connection, a fictional event, and a simulation result should never be indistinguishable.

**Give tiny projects their due.** Credit the people who built them and retain links to the original work.

**Respect licenses and safety.** Publicly visible code isn't automatically reusable. Check the relevant code, assets, datasets, and model licenses. Inspect and sandbox unfamiliar code before running it.

**Preserve failures.** An unsuccessful combination may teach us more than an obvious success.

**Let either branch surprise the other.** A practical tool could become the heart of a game mechanic. A fictional interaction could reveal a worthwhile real invention.

**Never confuse an expanding catalogue with progress on a playable game or working instrument.** Both are valuable, but they are different achievements.

## The two questions that will keep this alive

### A — What if absolutely everything could become part of a game?

Not as a trivia entry, but as a system, object, character obsession, environmental rule, mystery, puzzle, or consequence. A coherent experience made out of wildly incoherent ingredients: **serious in its mechanics, ridiculous in its imagination**.

### B — What if absolutely anything could become a useful invention?

Not a single prescribed product, but a workshop where someone else's odd 80-line script meets an ancient dataset, a scientific algorithm, a physical printer, or a musical instrument—and something genuinely new comes out.

We can make both. We can make neither. We can find a third answer by accident.

**F-round is permission to go very, very far off the rails—and still build things that actually work.**
